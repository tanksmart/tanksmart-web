Charging an electric car in Europe: prices, payment and planning

Checked on 26 November 2026

For fuel, the EU publishes average prices for every member state each week. For charging, the EU's Alternative Fuels Observatory publishes prices per country only occasionally, and its data was last updated in January 2024. That's why on TankSmart you won't find a table of charging prices per country. On this page you'll find how charging is priced, what EU rules guarantee you at a public charger, and how to plan a trip so that you pay less and wait less. At the bottom there is a map of charging points.

How the price is made up

At a public charger you can pay in several ways, and one charge can include more than one of them:

  • Per kWh: you pay for the energy that goes into the car. Under EU rules, the price without a contract at fast chargers of 50 kW or more has to be based on kWh.
  • Per minute: some operators charge for time, mostly at slower chargers. A car that can only take power slowly then pays more for the same amount of energy.
  • Per session: a fixed amount for each charge, used by some operators and apps.
  • Blocking or parking fee: a charge per minute if you stay plugged in too long or leave the car there after charging. In tariffs offered through third-party apps and charging cards, the consumer organisation BEUC found such fees in about 9 out of 10 cases.

Slow chargers use alternating current (AC), usually up to 22 kW, and are common in towns and car parks. Fast chargers use direct current (DC), usually 50 kW or more, and are what you mostly find along motorways. Fast charging usually costs more per kWh. According to figures from the EU's Alternative Fuels Observatory that BEUC quoted in July 2026 (the data itself may be older), average prices through apps and charging cards range from about 0.27 to 0.65 €/kWh for AC and from about 0.32 to 0.85 €/kWh for DC, and some prices are above 1 €/kWh.

What EU rules guarantee you

Since 13 April 2024 the EU regulation on alternative fuels infrastructure (AFIR) has applied in all EU countries. For drivers it means:

  • You can charge at any public charger without a contract and without registering in an app. In practice, some older chargers don't offer an easy way to pay without a contract yet.
  • New chargers of 50 kW or more must accept payment cards, through a card reader or a contactless terminal.
  • From 1 January 2027 this also applies to older chargers of 50 kW or more along the trans-European road network (TEN-T) and in secure truck parking areas.
  • At chargers below 50 kW the operator can let you pay online instead of by card, for example through a QR code.
  • At chargers of 50 kW or more the price per kWh without a contract must be shown at the charger. On top of the kWh price, the operator may only add a fee per minute for occupying the charger.
  • Apps and charging cards must show you all parts of the price, including roaming costs, before you start charging.

AFIR is an EU regulation. Outside the EU, for example in Switzerland, Serbia or the UK, it doesn't apply, so check how you can pay there before the trip.

Bank card, operator's app or charging card?

The same charger can cost very different amounts depending on how you pay. BEUC looked at data from more than 11,000 EU charging stations: the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive app or card at the same charger was usually 0.40 to 1.00 €/kWh.

  • Bank card at the charger: no contract and no app, which makes it the simplest option on a trip. You pay the price shown at the charger.
  • The operator's own app: usually gives you the operator's own price. In a BEUC example from the Netherlands, 51.4 kWh at Shell chargers cost 12.81 € through the operator, while a session of 53.7 kWh through another provider's app cost 45.90 €.
  • Charging cards and apps that work across many countries (roaming): convenient, because one account works with many networks, but the price can be higher than the operator's own and often includes fees per minute or for blocking. Always check the price in the app before you plug in.
  • A subscription can pay off if you often charge with the same network. Before you sign up, compare the monthly fee with how much you actually charge there.

Watch out for fake QR codes

ADAC warns about a scam at chargers: criminals stick their own QR code over the operator's code. It leads to a copy of the operator's website, where you enter your card details. If the charger's display shows a QR code, scan that one instead of the sticker. Check that no sticker has been put over another one, and be careful if the page asks for an unusually high amount or looks unprofessional. ADAC considers the operator's app or the card terminal at the charger the safer way to pay. Tesla says that it never asks customers to pay through a QR code.

Tesla Superchargers

Tesla has opened selected Supercharger sites to other electric cars in selected countries. You start and pay for charging in the Tesla app, and at a limited number of sites you can also pay directly with a card. A paid Supercharging membership lowers the price per kWh at selected sites. Idle and congestion fees apply to other cars too. Tesla's map shows which sites are open.

Planning a trip

  • Arrive at a fast charger with 10 to 30 percent battery and charge to about 80 percent. After 80 percent the charging power drops a lot, so the last part takes much longer (ADAC).
  • A cold battery charges much more slowly. Most cars warm it up on the way if you set the charger as the destination in the car's navigation (ADAC).
  • Winter means less range. In the Green NCAP test, electric cars used on average 70 percent more energy at -7 °C than at 23 °C in a short 23 km test cycle, and their range was about 40 percent lower. In ADAC's long-term tests, consumption in winter was 25 to 31 percent higher.
  • Heated seats and a heated steering wheel use less energy than warming the whole cabin. If you warm the car up while it's still plugged in, the energy comes from the grid and not from the battery (ADAC).
  • When charging is done, move the car. You avoid blocking fees, and someone else can charge.
  • Before you drive into a country outside the EU, check which networks and payment methods work there.

What does 100 km cost?

For an electric car, multiply consumption in kWh per 100 km by the price per kWh. For a petrol or diesel car, multiply litres per 100 km by the price per litre.

An example with round numbers: a car that uses 18 kWh per 100 km and charges at 0.60 €/kWh pays 10.80 € per 100 km. A petrol car that uses 6.5 litres per 100 km pays 10.40 € at 1.60 €/l. Charge at 0.40 €/kWh and the electric car pays 7.20 €.

Keep in mind that the consumption shown by the car usually doesn't include charging losses, and you pay for the energy measured at the charger. ADAC measured losses of 1 to 4 percent at fast chargers with a warm battery and 6 to 10 percent with a cold one. For fuel, use the current prices on TankSmart.
