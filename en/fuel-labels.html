E5, E10, B7, HVO: fuel labels at European pumps explained

Checked on 5 November 2026

At a foreign petrol station the names on the pumps can be confusing. In Italy diesel is called gasolio, in France gazole and in Czechia nafta. Luckily, since 2018 most of Europe uses the same labels, so you can tell what goes into your car even if you don't understand a word.

The shapes: circle, square, diamond

The labels follow the European standard EN 16942. They have been mandatory since October 2018 in the EU, Norway, Iceland, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Serbia, North Macedonia and Turkey. You'll find them on the pump, on the nozzle, inside the filler flap of newer cars and in the owner's manual.

  • Circle: petrol (E5, E10, E85)
  • Square: diesel (B7, B10, XTL)
  • Diamond: gas (LPG, CNG, LNG, H2)
  • Hexagon: electric charging (from a separate standard, EN 17186, used since about 2021)

The rule is simple: the label in your filler flap must match the label on the nozzle.

Petrol: E5, E10 and E85

The number tells you the maximum share of bioethanol. E5 contains up to 5 percent, E10 up to 10 percent. E85 contains much more and is only for special flex-fuel cars, so never put it in a normal car.

Can my car use E10?

Most can. According to the UK government, every new car made since 2011 is compatible, and so are most cars from the late 1990s on. The Austrian club ÖAMTC says practically all petrol cars from 2005 on are fine. Problems are mainly possible with some older and classic cars.

To check, look at the label inside the filler flap or in the owner's manual, look your car up in the compatibility list of the European car makers' association ACEA, or ask your dealer.

If your car can't take E10, fill up with 98 or 100 octane petrol, which is often still E5. One tank of E10 by mistake shouldn't damage the engine, according to the UK government and the Swedish fuel industry association. The Austrian club ÖAMTC still advises having part of a full tank drained, or topping up with E5 as soon as you can. Just don't make it a habit.

Where E10 is the standard 95

According to the German fuel and bioethanol industry associations (July 2026), E10 is sold in 21 countries: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Ireland, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Romania, Slovakia, Sweden and the UK. In many of them, such as Austria, Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, Finland, Hungary, Ireland, the Netherlands, Poland, Sweden and the UK, standard 95 is now usually E10. E5 is often still sold as 98 or 100 octane, but not everywhere, so check the label on the nozzle. Germany and France still sell both.

In Croatia, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Switzerland and the countries of the Western Balkans, standard 95 is still E5. For Slovenia the sources disagree, so check the label on the pump.

Diesel: B7, B10 and XTL (HVO)

B7 is normal diesel with up to 7 percent biodiesel and is sold everywhere. B10 contains up to 10 percent and is sold at some stations in France and Germany. Many car makers don't approve it, so only use it if your car allows it.

XTL means paraffinic diesel that isn't made from crude oil, for example HVO from waste fats and vegetable oils (standard EN 15940). Pure HVO100 has been sold at German pumps since May 2024 and is common in Scandinavia, the Netherlands, Italy and Austria. It's only for cars approved for it, which have an XTL label in the filler flap. These are mostly newer cars, often from around 2020 on, but it depends on the model, so check with the manufacturer if you're unsure.

Gas

LPG (autogas), CNG and LNG (natural gas) and H2 (hydrogen) are marked with a diamond. Each needs its own kind of car and its own nozzle, so you can't mix them up by accident.

Watch out for false friends

  • Nafta means diesel in Czech and Slovak, and naftë in Albanian. In Slovenia, Croatia and Serbia people also often say nafta for diesel. In Polish, nafta is paraffin, and diesel is ON.
  • Gasolio (Italy), gazole or gasoil (France), gasóleo (Spain, Portugal) and gázolaj (Hungary) are all diesel, not petrol.
  • In Greek, πετρέλαιο (petréleo) means diesel. Petrol is αμόλυβδη (amólyvdi), which means unleaded.
  • Super means 95 octane in Germany and Austria, and Super Plus is 98. In the UK, Super Unleaded is premium petrol with 97 octane or more.
  • Plavi dizel in Croatia is blue-dyed, lower-tax diesel for farming and commercial fishing. Using it in a car is illegal.
  • In Slovenia, Petrol is the name of a company, not a type of fuel.
